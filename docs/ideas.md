# Ideas

A working document, not a plan. Things worth building next, what each one would
cost, and — more usefully — which of them break something the codebase currently
guarantees. Nothing here is committed to.

> **`docs/roadmap.md` is where things get committed to.** It takes the order at
> the end of this file, groups it into milestones M8 onwards (continuing the
> numbering `docs/plan.md` and the tuning journal already use), and answers
> several of the open questions below. This file keeps the reasoning for why they
> were hard; the roadmap records what was chosen. Where the two disagree, the
> roadmap is the decision and this is the argument.

Two references, and they are not the same one despite both saying "Horizon".
Worth separating up front, because everything below leans on one or the other:

- **The mode is Forza Horizon's time attack.** A clean solo lap against your own
  ghost, split deltas, medals to chase, a garage to pick a car from. Not a
  simulator, not a career with cutscenes — the loop is *drive the lap again, two
  tenths faster*.
- **The look is Horizon Chase.** Vivid flat-shaded colour blocking, no textures,
  enormous graphic skies, a dramatic time of day per circuit, and a horizon full
  of silhouettes. Bright and readable, never grimy.

The second one is a decision, not an option, and section 5 is written to it.

## Where the game is now

Three shipped circuits plus anything the player draws, one car, sixteen ordered
gates, a lap record per track, and a trackside that dresses itself. Handling is
measured rather than guessed. There is **no audio at all**, no opponents, no
ghost, no progression, and one vehicle.

The good news for everything below is structural: `TrackBuilder` already turns a
layout into a circuit for both the tool and the game, lap timing accumulates in
`_physics_process` so a headless run reproduces a real time, and all feel
parameters already live in a swappable `CarTuning` resource rather than on
nodes.

## 1. The pit lane as the garage

The idea: drive into the pit lane, pull into a bay, and that is how you change
car. No menu screen — the selector *is* a place on the track.

**The art already exists and is already vendored.** `roadPitEntry`,
`roadPitStraight`, `roadPitStraightLong` and `roadPitGarage` are all in
`assets/kenney/racing_kit/`, along with `roadSplit*` pieces for branching off the
main road. `pitsGarage` and `pitsOffice` are already being placed by the paddock
code in `_scenery_paddock`.

**The problem is topology, not art.** `TrackShape.walk` is the single definition
of a valid circuit, and it defines one *simple closed ring* — `_accept` refuses
any edit that leaves anything else. `TrackLayout.compile` calls the same `walk`,
so the editor and the compiler can never disagree. A pit lane is a branch and a
merge, which is precisely the thing that invariant exists to forbid. Do not
casually widen it: that invariant is why a broken circuit cannot be built in the
first place.

Two ways out:

- **(a) Don't make it part of the circuit.** Generate the pit lane the way the
  paddock is generated — from the finished centreline, parallel to the pit
  straight, as tiles plus its own strip of collision ribbon plus an `Area3D`.
  The main loop stays one simple ring and `TrackShape` never learns about it.
  This works today because grass grips like tarmac and there are no walls, so
  "leave the road and drive up the pit lane" is already a thing the car can do.
  **Recommended first.** It is mostly a variation on scenery placement, which now
  exists.
- **(b) Model it properly as a branch.** `TrackShape` learns about a second path
  with an entry and an exit, `TrackLayout` learns to emit it, closure maths grows
  a special case, and the editor grows a way to draw it. Much more honest, much
  more work, and it puts the safest part of the codebase at risk. Worth doing
  only if the pit lane needs to be *drawable*, not merely present.

**What the car selector itself needs.** More than it looks. `car.tscn` currently
holds one car's geometry, generated by `tools/build_car.gd` from `race.glb` with
its wheel positions, radii and collision box hard-coded — and those numbers are
what `docs/tuning-journal.md` measured the handling against. The kit has about
twenty other models (`sedan-sports`, `police`, `taxi`, `van`, `truck`,
`race-future`, five karts, a tractor) and **they are all different sizes**. So:

- A `CarSpec` resource: display name, source `.glb`, wheel positions, wheel
  radius, collision extents, and a `CarTuning` to go with it. This is the natural
  extension of the decision that already put feel in a resource instead of on
  nodes.
- `tools/build_car.gd` generalised to bake one scene per spec, rather than one
  scene from constants.
- `GameState.selected_car`, alongside `selected_index`, and `race.gd` instancing
  the chosen one.
- A tuning pass per car. This is the real cost. A kart and a truck sharing
  `grippy.tres` will feel like the same vehicle in different clothes, which
  defeats the point of having a garage.

**One decision to make early: what a lap record is keyed on.** Records are
currently `best_<track_id>` (`GameState.record_key`). The moment there are cars
of different speeds, one record per track stops meaning anything. Either key on
track *and* car, or nominate a single spec car that leaderboard times are set in
and treat the rest as free play. The first is more honest; the second keeps the
title screen simple. Decide before writing the save format, not after.

**And a scale question that is about to get sharper.** The road is 14 m wide but
the car is only 2.56 m long — the kit is drawn to its own proportions and this
project stretched the tiles without stretching the car. It is already why
trackside props had to be sized against the car rather than the tiles (see
`docs/architecture.md`). Add a truck and a kart to the same road and the
mismatch stops being subtle. Either scale the whole car kit up by ~2x and re-tune,
or accept it and never show two very different vehicles side by side.

## 2. The editor as the other half of the game

Drawing circuits should be a headline feature, not a bonus mode. It is already
the most novel thing here — a player-drawn track compiles to exactly the same
collision ribbon, ordered gates and generated scenery as Ardennes does, because
one builder serves both. The gap is not capability, it is **reach**: circuits
have nowhere to go, and the drawing tool tells you almost nothing about what you
have drawn.

### Sharing is the missing half

Custom tracks are stored as JSON under `user://tracks/`, and that was chosen
deliberately — a Godot resource file can name a script to attach, which is the
wrong shape for a file players swap. The decision was made *for* sharing and
sharing was never built.

On the web export there is no filesystem to hand someone a file from; `user://`
is browser storage. So the practical form is a **share code**: the layout JSON,
deflate-compressed and base64'd, copied to the clipboard and pasted back on the
other side. A circuit becomes a string you can put in a message. Godot has all
the pieces already (`PackedByteArray.compress`, `Marshalls.raw_to_base64`), and
it works identically on desktop and web, which nothing filesystem-based would.

**Then attach the ghost.** Share a circuit *with* your best lap on it, so whoever
opens it is racing your time from their first attempt rather than an empty track.
That is the feature that turns a drawing tool into something people send each
other, and given ghosts (section 4) it is close to free.

### Crossings: the biggest expressive win still inside the grid

`roadCrossing.glb` is sitting unused in the kit, and the bridge pieces
(`roadStraightBridge`, `roadCornerBridge*`) are already used for elevated
sections. `TrackShape._accept` currently refuses any loop that touches itself,
which is right *today* — two legs at the same height are one road, not two.

But elevation is already an absolute level per segment. A circuit that crosses
over itself, where one leg is raised and the other is not, is a perfectly
legitimate circuit — Le Mans has the Dunlop bridge, Suzuka is a figure of eight.
Teaching `walk` that a self-touch is permitted **when the two visits sit at
different levels**, and having the compiler emit a crossing or a bridge there,
would unlock a whole class of layouts that cannot currently be drawn at all.

It is the sharpest available change: one carefully-scoped relaxation of an
invariant, with the art and the elevation system already in place to support it.
It is also the one that most needs its own tests, because `_accept` refusing
invalid shapes is what makes the editor safe.

### Don't abandon the grid

Tempting to add diagonals — `roadStraightSkew` exists. Resist it for now. The
rectilinear model is *why* closure is arithmetic instead of trial and error, and
why every edit can be checked for validity before it is accepted. Diagonals mean
a new polygon model, a new closure proof, and a new definition of a valid loop.
There is a lot of unexplored room inside the grid first: crossings, longer
elevation chains, tighter chicanes.

### Tell the designer what they have drawn

The editor recompiles on every mouse move and already calls `measure()`, which
returns length, corner count and peak height for nothing. Almost none of that
reaches the screen. Surface it live while drawing:

- longest straight, tightest corner, total elevation change
- an **estimated lap time** — the same par-time function the medals would use
- a nudge when a circuit is pathological: fourteen hairpins and no straight, or
  a lap so short the gates end up metres apart

That turns a drawing tool into a design tool, and it costs a panel, not an
architecture.

### Stamps

A hairpin, a chicane, an Esses dropped in as one gesture. Harder than it sounds
and worth being honest about: any stamp has to leave a single closed ring, so it
must consume and emit straight length the way corner radius already does
(a size-N corner spends N-1 cells either side). Probably the right shape is
"stamp into an existing straight, reduced to fit" — the same *reduce, don't
refuse* rule elevation already follows.

### Scenery themes

Now that the trackside generates itself from the centreline, a theme is a colour
palette plus a prop table: forest, coastal, desert, night. Cheap to add, and it
is the difference between two player circuits looking like different places and
looking like the same field twice.

### Ordinary editor comfort

Undo and redo (the shape is a value — snapshot it), duplicating a shipped
circuit as a starting point instead of always beginning from the default
rectangle, mirroring or rotating a whole loop, and renaming. Test-driving already
works and returns to the editor with the edit intact, which is the hardest one of
these and it is done.

### A real library

Custom circuits currently appear on the title screen with **Edit** and
**Delete**. Eventually they want a browser of their own: sort by name or date,
best time and medal per circuit, and a thumbnail — which is nearly free, because
`track_grid.gd` already draws the compiled circuit rather than the painted cells,
so it can render one at any size.

## 3. Progression

The risk here is building a grind. This is a time attack game; the reward for
driving well should be *driving well*, and locking the interesting cars behind
twenty laps of a circuit you have already beaten is how that gets ruined. So:

**Medals, not currency.** Gold / silver / bronze per circuit, per car. The target
times can be *derived* rather than authored: `TrackBuilder.measure()` already
returns length, corner count and peak height without instancing a single tile —
`GameState.describe` uses it to write "1473 m, 14 corners" on the title screen.
A par time is a function of those numbers plus the car's top speed, which means
**player-drawn circuits get medals too**, for free. That is a much better story
than "progression exists only on the three tracks we shipped".

Sketch:

- `par = length / effective_average_speed(corners, peak, car)` — calibrate the
  constant against the three shipped circuits, whose real times are known.
- Gold at par, silver at par x 1.06, bronze at par x 1.15. Numbers to be measured,
  like everything else here.
- Storage next to the records, and **overridable the same way**:
  `GameState.records_path` is already swapped by the test suite so it never
  touches real saves. Anything new must preserve that.

**What medals could unlock**, in increasing order of how much they risk the loop:
new cars (fine, if everything is driveable from the start and medals unlock
*variety* rather than *capability*), new circuits (fine), liveries and cosmetics
(fine), performance upgrades (avoid — it makes old lap times incomparable, which
is the one thing a time attack game cannot afford).

## 4. Time attack features that are nearly free

These are the highest value per line of code, because the infrastructure already
exists.

**Ghost cars.** The single best thing this game could gain. Lap timing already
accumulates in `_physics_process` specifically so headless runs reproduce real
times — which means recording the car's transform every physics tick and playing
it back is straightforward and deterministic. At 60 Hz a two-minute lap is about
7,200 samples; as position plus quaternion that is under 250 KB uncompressed, and
far less if only the delta from the previous sample is stored. Save it next to
the lap record, replay it as a translucent car, and every session becomes a race
against the best version of yourself.

**Sector splits, from gates that already exist.** There are sixteen ordered
checkpoints and `lap_tracker` already enforces their order. Recording the time at
each one turns them into sixteen free sector times, and comparing against the
stored best gives a **live delta** — the "+0.42 / -0.18" readout that makes a
time attack game addictive. This is a small change to `lap_tracker.gd` and a HUD
label, and it needs no new geometry whatsoever.

**Speed traps and drift zones** reuse the same `Area3D` machinery: a gate that
records the speed through it, or a stretch that scores lateral slip. Horizon's
whole side-activity layer is built from exactly this.

## 5. Look and feel: the Horizon Chase direction

The target is **Horizon Chase** — vivid, flat-shaded, textureless colour blocking;
huge graphic skies with an oversized sun and banded clouds; a strong time of day
per circuit; dense roadside objects streaming past; a low, close camera. Bright,
saturated and instantly readable. Never grimy, never photoreal.

That is a good fit for what already exists. The Kenney kit is untextured
flat-shaded geometry, which is the expensive half of this look already done, and
it survives the compatibility renderer the web build is stuck with. What is
missing is **saturation, sky, and drama** — not detail.

> **This supersedes some earlier instincts in this document.** An earlier draft
> suggested clearcoat car paint, sky reflections, road wear and patches, and
> making the ground plane fully lit. Those pull towards realism and are the wrong
> direction for this target. Where they conflict, this section wins. Each one is
> called out below rather than quietly deleted, because the reasoning for the
> reversal is the useful part.

### Colour first, and it is nearly free

The single highest-leverage change, and it needs no new art. Kenney's palette is
**pastel** — the grass is a mint green, the kerbs are near-white, the car is a
soft orange. Horizon Chase is vivid: deep saturated blues, hot oranges, punchy
greens.

Godot's `Environment` has exactly the tool: `adjustment_enabled` with
`adjustment_saturation`, `adjustment_contrast`, `adjustment_brightness`, and
`adjustment_color_correction` for a full gradient or LUT. `_build_lighting`
already constructs the `Environment` programmatically, so this is a handful of
properties. A per-circuit LUT is then how Ardennes, Monte Carlo and La Sarthe
come to look like different *places* rather than the same field three times —
and it costs one small texture each, not a new art pass.

Do this before anything else in this section. It changes every screenshot.

### The sky is a main character

Horizon Chase skies are half the frame and carry the mood. Right now the sky is a
stock `ProceduralSkyMaterial` with tuned colours — fine, and forgettable.

- Push the existing one much harder first: a far more saturated top colour, a
  bigger and brighter sun disc via `sun_angle_max` and `sun_curve`.
- Then replace it with a **custom sky shader**: gradient bands, a huge sun, and
  flat stylised cloud bands. Sky shaders are cheap, self-contained, need no
  assets, and are the most Horizon Chase thing available. This is where the look
  is won or lost.
- Fog colour should track the sky per preset. That already happens; it just needs
  to follow the presets once they exist.

### Time of day per circuit

In Horizon Chase every race has its own hour — sunset, night, dawn, storm — and
that is most of why the circuits feel distinct. `_build_lighting` builds the sun
and environment in code, so a preset is a small struct: sun angle, sun colour and
energy, sky gradient, fog colour, and the colour grade above.

Attach a preset to the circuit rather than putting it behind a menu. Player-drawn
circuits pick one, which gives the editor another meaningful choice to offer.

**Night is the one worth building for specifically.** It is the most striking
preset, and it is the reason to finish the lighting columns that are already
being placed trackside — they currently cast no light at all.

### The car

- **Not clearcoat, not reflections.** An earlier draft suggested breaking the
  disabled sky reflections for the car. Wrong target: Horizon Chase cars are flat
  colour with dark glass and a hard silhouette. Leave
  `reflected_light_source` disabled — it stays cheap *and* correct.
- **A rim light** instead. A fresnel edge term picking up the sky colour separates
  the car from the road at speed and is the one lighting trick this style does
  use. Cheap in a shader, no reflection probe needed.
- **Saturated single colours** per car, straight out of the palette atlas. The
  garage becomes a colour choice as much as a shape choice.
- **Brake lights** that actually glow, and a simple shadow — see below.

### The road

- **Flatter, not dirtier.** The earlier suggestion of wear, patches and seams is
  the wrong direction. Horizon Chase roads are clean flat colour with crisp,
  high-contrast markings. The grain in `tarmac.gdshader` is currently subtle and
  probably fine, but the direction of travel is **less** surface noise, not more —
  and if the grade above pushes contrast up, the grain may want dialling back
  rather than building on.
- **Markings are the detail.** Bold white edge lines, and red-and-white kerbs,
  matter far more here than surface texture. The caveat from before still stands
  and is worth repeating: the painted lines and the grid boxes share the `grey`
  surface with the kerbs, so recolouring `grey` wholesale repaints the markings
  too. It needs a kerb-only surface or a shader that keys off geometry.
- **Rubber on the racing line** is still worth it — it reads as a graphic band,
  not as grime, which suits the style fine. Still needs the ribbon to carry a
  lateral coordinate in `UV2` or vertex colour.

### Ground and shadows — a correction

An earlier item called making the ground plane lit "probably the largest single
visual improvement available". For a photoreal target that is true. For this one
it is half wrong, and the distinction matters:

`ground_grid.gdshader` is `unshaded`, which keeps the grass flat and bright — and
**flat and bright is the Horizon Chase look**. Making it fully lit would sink the
grass into shading gradients this style does not want.

What is actually missing is not global shading but **a shadow under the car**. The
car currently casts onto the road and onto nothing else, so the moment it runs
wide it appears to float. The right fix is a simple blob or projected shadow that
works regardless of what is underneath, not a lit ground plane. Grandstand
shadows are a separate, lesser question — a soft darkening baked into the
scenery would be more in keeping than real shadow maps.

Same reasoning for **SSAO**: it was suggested earlier, and it is a realism tool.
Skip it. Contact is better sold here by clean silhouettes and that one reliable
shadow.

### The horizon, and the sense of speed

- **Silhouettes on the horizon.** Mountains, a skyline, a band of palms. This is a
  signature of the style and the world currently just fades into fog. A ring of
  cheap geometry beyond the fog distance, coloured by the time-of-day preset, does
  it.
- **Density is speed.** Horizon Chase sells speed with a *lot* of roadside
  objects streaming past. The scenery system already places trees from a density
  constant and already keeps props off the road — raising the density and adding
  repeated signs and posts is largely a matter of tuning what exists.
- **Low and close camera.** `CarTuning` already carries the follow offset, look
  offset, lag and a speed-scaled FOV kick. This style wants the camera lower and
  nearer than a simulator would, with a wide FOV that widens further with speed.
  All of that is already parameterised — it needs a pass, not a feature.
- **Lead the corner** with a slight camera yaw toward where the car is going.
- **Undulating ground** stays a good idea: the collision ribbon is generated
  independently of the ground plane, so the grass can roll for looks without
  touching what the car drives on.

### Set dressing, and weather as colour

- **Weather is a colour treatment here, not a simulation.** Horizon Chase does
  rain, fog and snow as strong tinted overlays with a matching sky, rather than as
  wet surfaces and spray. That is much cheaper and much more in keeping: it lands
  as another time-of-day-style preset — sky, fog colour, grade — plus a lighter
  touch on the tarmac shader's roughness. If it also lowers the grip constants in
  `CarTuning` it becomes a genuine gameplay variant rather than a filter, which is
  worth doing for at least one of them.
- **Crowds and flags** on the grandstands that now exist. Flat, bold and
  repeated — a crowd here is a texture of coloured dots, not geometry, and the
  `flagGreen` / `flagRed` / `flagCheckers` pieces are already vendored and unused.
- **A start light sequence on the gantry**, whose three screens finally face the
  grid. This is both set dressing and the missing beat before a timed lap begins.
- **Scenery themes** — forest, coastal, desert, night — pair naturally with the
  time-of-day presets, since both are a palette plus a prop table. One choice in
  the editor could set both.

### The controls

Unchanged by the art direction, and still the most under-served part of the game.

- **Throttle and brake are binary.** `car_controller` reads steering with
  `Input.get_axis`, which is analogue-aware, but throttle, brake and handbrake all
  go through `Input.is_action_pressed`. **A gamepad's analogue triggers are being
  thrown away entirely** — half-throttle out of a hairpin is impossible on any
  device today. Switching those three to `Input.get_action_strength` and scaling
  `engine_force` and `brake` by it is a small change with a large effect, and it
  needs a re-measure afterwards because partial throttle invalidates every
  corner-exit figure in the tuning journal.
  *(Worth noting the tension: Horizon Chase itself is arcade and largely binary.
  Analogue control is the Forza half of the brief. Both can be true — analogue
  input under an arcade handling model — but if the game ever feels fussier to
  drive as a result, this is the thing to reconsider.)*
- **A steering curve.** The stick maps linearly and is then smoothed by
  `steer_speed`, which fights an analogue input. A response curve with less lerp
  gives fine control near centre with full lock still reachable.
- **Rumble.** Nothing calls `Input.start_joy_vibration`. Kerbs, wheelspin, grass
  and contact are all already detected.
- **The touch pads are binary too**, and a variable control is harder there —
  worth deciding whether the phone build gets a slider-style throttle.
- **Assists** — countersteer help, gentle off-track correction — act on input
  rather than on the physics, so they widen the audience without touching the
  handling model. Very much in keeping with an arcade racer.

### Audio, in this style

Horizon Chase leans on a bright chiptune-adjacent soundtrack as much as on engine
noise. There is currently **no audio at all**, which remains the single biggest
gap in the project. Engine pitch from wheel RPM and tyre squeal from the skid
values every wheel already reports, plus music that matches the palette.

## 6. Surfaces: dirt, snow, and tracks with depth

Two separable features that get talked about as one: **what the surface is** —
grip, colour, sound — and **what the tyres do to it**. The first is
straightforward and mostly already plumbed. The second is the ambitious one, and
this codebase happens to have an unusually good angle on it.

### There is no dirt or snow art, and that turns out not to matter

Checked: the kit has no dirt, snow or gravel *road* pieces. The `*Sand` variants
(`roadCornerLargeSand`, `roadCornerSmallSand` and friends) carry a single `sand`
material and no `road` surface at all — they are **run-off aprons** that sit
outside a corner, not a dirt road.

That is fine, because the drivable surface is already re-materialised at load
time. `TrackBuilder.surface_road` walks the tiles and replaces every surface
Kenney names `road` with the tarmac shader. **Dirt and snow are shader variants
swapped through that same hook** — no new geometry, no new tiles, and the tarmac
shader is the template to copy. The mechanism was built for something else and
lands exactly where this needs it.

The sand aprons are then a free bonus: gravel traps on the outside of fast
corners, placed by the scenery system, which already knows how to put things at a
lateral offset from the centreline and keep them off other legs of the circuit.

### Where a surface type lives

The layout already carries **elevation per segment** and **banking per corner**.
Surface is the natural third property of the same kind, cycled in the editor the
way banking is cycled flat → 1.5° → 2.5° → 4°.

Two steps, and the first is much cheaper than it looks:

- **Per circuit.** A winter Ardennes, a dirt La Sarthe. One field on the layout,
  no editor work, no per-segment format change. This alone gets snow and dirt into
  the game and is the sane first move.
- **Per segment.** Where rally stages come from — tarmac onto gravel and back, a
  snowy section in the shade. More interesting, and it fits the existing editor
  idiom exactly, but it needs the transitions to look deliberate rather than like
  a seam.

### Grip, and what it breaks

`CarTuning` already carries `friction_front` and `friction_rear`. Surface-varying
grip means the car has to know what it is standing on. `_surface_up` already
raycasts down every physics frame, so the type could ride on the hit — but the
cheaper and more robust lookup is **by arc**: nearest centreline index → segment →
surface. No splitting the collision shape, and it works off data the builder
already has.

Then be honest about the consequences, because they are real:

- **Grass currently grips like tarmac, and that is load-bearing.** It is why
  cutting a corner is only discouraged by the ordered gates, and it is what makes
  the "just drive up the pit lane" approach in section 1 cheap. Introduce
  genuinely low-grip surfaces and both of those change.
- **Every number in the tuning journal is a tarmac number.** Each surface needs
  its own sweep — top speed, braking distance, corner speeds — or it will be a
  guess wearing measured clothes.
- **Lap records get another dimension.** A snow lap and a dry lap are not
  comparable, which joins the per-car question already open in section 1. Better
  to settle both at once than to bolt a second key on later.

### Tyre tracks with depth, in three steps

Each one is useful on its own and each is a prerequisite for the next.

**Step 1 — trail geometry.** Emit a quad strip on the ground behind each wheel
while `is_in_contact()` is true, shaded dark or light to read as a rut. No render
targets, no special renderer features, works everywhere including the web build,
and persists for as long as you keep the geometry — cap the length and recycle.
This is the cheapest thing that genuinely looks like tracks.

**Step 2 — a track-space deformation texture.** The good idea, and it is good
*because of what this project already is*.

The road is a **ribbon**, so it has a natural two-coordinate parameterisation:
distance along the lap, and offset across the road. Unwrap the entire circuit
into one texture on those axes. A 4096 x 128 target covers a 1500 m lap at
roughly 0.37 m along by 0.11 m across — fine for ruts. The world-space equivalent
is hopeless: a 500 m square at that resolution is a texture nobody can allocate.
The ribbon makes the problem small.

Then splat each wheel's contact point into that texture every frame and let it
**accumulate across laps**, so lap three shows the lines you cut on laps one and
two. That persistence is the part that actually feels good.

> **This is the same coordinate the racing-line rubber idea in section 5 needs.**
> Both want the ribbon to carry its lateral position per vertex, in `UV2` or
> vertex colour. Do it once, deliberately, and two features fall out of it. It is
> the single most reusable small change on this list.

**Step 3 — real depth.** Sample that texture in the vertex shader and push
vertices down; take the normal from its gradient in the fragment shader so the
ruts catch light properly.

This needs vertex density the Kenney tiles do not have — but generating a dense
visual ribbon is squarely inside what the builder already does. It generates the
collision ribbon from scratch, and `_reshape_tiles` already rewrites tile meshes
vertex by vertex for banking and elevation. Vertex texture fetch is guaranteed in
GLES3, so this survives the compatibility renderer the web build is stuck with.

### What depth can and cannot do to the physics

Worth deciding early and writing down. The collision surface is a single
`ConcavePolygonShape3D` built once at load. Rebuilding it per frame to match the
ruts is not realistic and not worth it.

So **the depth is visual**, and the physics reads the same texture as a *modifier*
rather than as geometry: fresh snow drags and pushes the car around, an existing
rut gives grip and a mild steering pull, a churned line is slower than clean
tarmac. That is what most games in this space actually do, and it delivers the
whole mechanic — cutting your own line and then following it — without touching
the collision shape at all.

### Snow and dirt in the Horizon Chase palette

Following section 5, these want to be **bold and graphic, not photoreal**:

- **Snow** as flat bright white with a strong blue-shadowed rut, not grey slush.
  Snow banks along the road edge, placed the way the barrier already is.
- **Dirt** as a saturated ochre with darker churned lines, and a lot of it kicked
  into the air.
- **Particles** from the wheels, tinted per surface, driven by the
  `get_skidinfo()` values every wheel already reports and the debug overlay
  already reads.
- **Pair each surface with a weather and time-of-day preset** from section 5. A
  snow circuit wants the snow sky and the cold grade; they are the same kind of
  data and should be chosen together.

### Constraints to check before designing around them

- **Decals are the obvious tool and are probably unavailable.** Godot 4's `Decal`
  node is a Forward+/Mobile feature and the web build runs Compatibility. Verify
  against 4.7 before relying on it — if it holds, steps 1 and 2 above are the
  routes that work on every target, which is a good reason to prefer them anyway.
- **Single-threaded web.** A small per-frame splat into a `SubViewport` is fine;
  anything that wants a worker thread is not.
- **The deformation texture is session state** and is not saved. Unless you want
  it to be — persisting the ruts alongside a best-lap ghost would be a genuinely
  nice touch, and it is the same file.

## 7. Things that will have to give

Honest list of what a "full game" would force a decision on.

- **The barriers do not collide.** `BARRIERS_ENABLED` is still `false`, and the
  swept barrier added for looks has no collision either. Nothing physically stops
  cutting a corner; only the ordered gates make a time mean anything. A game with
  medals and leaderboards probably wants real barriers, which means a wall
  collider following the same swept path, and a re-tune of how forgiving contact
  feels.
- **Grass grips like tarmac.** Convenient today, and it is what makes idea (a)
  for the pit lane cheap. It is also why running wide costs nothing.
- **No opponents.** Everything above is solo. AI traffic is a much larger project
  than any single item here and should not be smuggled in as a sub-task.
- **The web build constrains all of it.** Single-threaded, compatibility renderer,
  no system fonts. Ghost replay data, extra cars and audio all land in the same
  `.pck` that has to download in a browser. Any new UI text must stay inside the
  built-in font or it ships as a tofu box — the suite checks, but only for text
  it can see.

## A possible order

Sequenced so each step is useful on its own and nothing is blocked on a decision
that has not been made yet.

1. **Colour grade, and analogue throttle and brake.** The cheapest big win of
   each kind. A saturation and contrast pass on the `Environment` moves the whole
   game towards the Horizon Chase palette without touching an asset; switching
   throttle and brake to `get_action_strength` transforms how it feels to drive.
   Do both first. The throttle change needs a re-measure afterwards.
2. **Sector splits and live delta.** Smallest change, biggest immediate effect on
   how the game feels to play. No new decisions required.
3. **Editor readout** — length, longest straight, elevation, estimated lap time,
   live while drawing. A panel, not an architecture, and it makes the editor feel
   like a design tool immediately.
4. **Ghost cars.** Builds directly on 2 and on the determinism already designed
   for. This is the point at which it becomes a time attack game rather than a
   lap timer.
5. **Share codes**, then share codes *with a ghost attached*. This is what gives
   drawn circuits somewhere to go, and it is the reason the layouts were stored
   as JSON in the first place.
6. **Audio.** Nothing else makes the existing driving feel this much better.
7. **Crossings and bridges in the editor.** The biggest expressive unlock still
   inside the grid, and the one needing the most care around `TrackShape._accept`.
8. **`CarSpec` and a second car**, chosen from the title screen at first. Settles
   the record-keying question with one real example in front of you instead of in
   the abstract.
9. **The pit lane**, approach (a), as the place you change car — once there is
   more than one car to change to.
10. **Medals**, derived from `measure()` so custom circuits are included from day
   one rather than bolted on.
11. **A custom sky shader and time-of-day presets.** The point at which it
   stops looking like a Kenney demo and starts looking like Horizon Chase. Then
   horizon silhouettes, scenery themes and weather.
12. **Lateral coordinate on the ribbon.** Small, and it is the prerequisite for
   both racing-line rubber and the deformation texture. Worth doing on its own,
   before either.
13. **Surfaces per circuit** — a snow variant and a dirt variant, as shader swaps
   through the hook `surface_road` already provides, plus their own grip sweeps.
14. **Tyre tracks**, steps 1 to 3: trail geometry, then the track-space
   deformation texture accumulating across laps, then real vertex displacement.

## Open questions

- Is a lap record per car, or is there one spec car that counts?
- Should the pit lane be drawable in the editor, or generated for every circuit?
- Do medals apply to player-drawn circuits? (Deriving par times says yes, cheaply
   — but a player can draw a circuit that games the formula.)
- Does the car kit get scaled up to match the road, or does the road come down to
  match the car? This one gets harder to change the more cars exist.
- Is a self-crossing circuit allowed to cross at the *same* level (one road, two
  ways through it) or only at different levels? The second is far easier to
  reason about and to gate.
- Do shared circuits carry their author's ghost by default, or is that opt-in?
- How far does the Horizon Chase palette go — a global saturation push, or a
  hand-authored LUT per circuit? The second is much stronger and is also the point
  at which someone has to art-direct three separate looks.
- Does the arcade look imply arcade input? Horizon Chase is largely binary, and
  the analogue-throttle change is the Forza half of the brief pulling the other
  way.
- Does analogue throttle invalidate the existing lap records? Strictly it makes
  faster times possible, so probably yes — better decided before medals exist
  than after.
- Is a surface chosen per circuit or per segment? Per circuit is far cheaper and
  gets snow and dirt into the game; per segment is what makes rally stages
  possible and is where the editor idiom already points.
- If grass and gravel stop gripping like tarmac, what stops corner cutting — the
  ordered gates alone, as now, or do the barriers finally need collision?
- Are lap times comparable across surfaces? Almost certainly not, which makes the
  record-key question from section 1 really "per car *and* per surface".
- Should tyre tracks persist between sessions, saved next to the ghost, or reset
  each time the circuit loads?
- If a share code is a string, what stops someone editing it into a circuit the
  editor would never have accepted? `TrackLayout.compile` calls `walk` too, so an
  invalid one cannot build — but it should fail politely, not silently.
